TW-2 Ferret Turbine-Wheel

A two-seat machine that drives like a wide, low buggy and takes off from where it is parked. Not a helicopter with wheels. Not a car that flies. A ground vehicle whose wheels are its engines — and the moment that defines it is the six seconds in which those wheels stop being wheels.

Rev F is an architecture change, not an edit. The electric rotor-wheel of Revs A–E is replaced by a turbine-wheel: a gas-generator core lives in the hub of each wheel and drives the fan directly. The hull lift fan is deleted. The wheels gain a second degree of freedom — they slide outboard as well as rotate. And takeoff is no longer a tilt; it is a four-beat transformation that is now the signature of the product.

Design principles

  1. 01The wheel is the product. Everything else — hull, canopy, tail — is service volume between four identical modules. If a feature does not serve the module or the people, it is deleted.
  2. 02Tilt is the throttle. The cores run at constant speed. Lift is commanded by rotating thrust, not by fuelling it. This is what makes a turbine viable in a machine that must hover.
  3. 03Narrow on the road, wide in the air. 92 inches across to drive, 136 to fly. The transformation earns both.
  4. 04Spool before you need it. Nothing time-critical happens to the engines during takeoff. They are already running, already hot, already stable.
  5. 05Show the state. A bystander 50 feet away should read what the machine is about to do from its posture alone. Orange marks everything that moves or gets hot.

02The transformation

The hero interaction. Four beats, about six seconds from spooled to airborne.

{{FIG_TRANS}}
BEAT 01

Spool

Cores start and stabilise at flight idle with the wheels still vertical. Thrust runs inboard into the flank channels and turns aft — ground thrust, zero lift, lateral halves cancelling.

moves: nothing · ~20 s
BEAT 02

Roll-in

Each wheel's top tips inboard. Thrust swings from horizontal toward down-and-inboard; the vertical component builds as sin θ and the suspension unloads to ~62% of static.

moves: roll collars, 0 → 38°
BEAT 03

Slide-out

Modules translate outboard, clearing the disc from the hull before it can go flat and widening the track from 92 to 136 inches.

moves: slide rails, 0 → 22 in · ~1.5 s
BEAT 04

Roll-out

Rotation completes. Discs flat, inflow clean, thrust fully vertical. The vehicle leaves the ground.

moves: roll collars, 38 → 95°

Why this order, and not another

The disc is 52 inches across and the hull is 60 wide. A wheel rotated flat at its drive-mode track would overlap the hull by 10 inches a side — blowing on the body, losing thrust and ingesting its own recirculation. The slide is not drama; it is the only way to be narrow standing still and wide in the air. And it has to happen between the two rotations: roll in to unload the tires, slide while partly unloaded, then finish the roll in clear air. Landing reverses it — settle wide, roll in to 38°, slide in, roll down onto the tires.

What the operator does. One two-stage detented lever, not a button. Stage one arms and spools; stage two commits, and the transformation runs closed-loop — there is no partial state the vehicle will sit in. In flight the same lever commands tilt angle, and the pilot can watch the wheels answer it. The control surface of this vehicle is visible from outside.

Sound. The cores never change speed, so the transformation has no rev. The note stays constant and the direction changes — a swell, not a climb. A vehicle that sounds the same taking off as it does idling is unusual, and worth protecting in detail design.

03Form & stance

Proportion. Wheel diameter is 60 inches on a 198-inch vehicle — 30% of overall length, roughly double a truck's — and the wheels stand level with the roof. That is the whole visual argument: the machine reads as four engines with a body slung between them, because that is what it is.

The shoulder line is not styling. It is the slide-rail housing, a structural extrusion running the length of the body that carries the modules outboard. Giving the strongest horizontal line in the design a job is the central form decision: the thing that looks like a feature line is the thing that moves the wheels.

The flank is a working surface. Between the wheels each side is sculpted into a reaction channel — a shaped duct that catches the inboard fan flow while the wheels are still vertical and turns it aft. A spooled turbine on the ground pushes the vehicle forward instead of blowing dust sideways. The body sides do aerodynamic work, which is why they are shaped and not skinned.

04The turbine-wheel module

Four identical modules — one part number, mounted mirrored left and right so the fans counter-rotate and reaction torques cancel. From the outside in: a printed TPU airless tire ring; a rim drive motor for ground running, silent creep and emergency lift boost; the structural rim, which is the duct wall; a 12-blade variable-pitch fan; a ~15:1 reduction gearbox at the hub; and the gas-generator core, which protrudes outboard behind a faired spinner and exhausts through the hub centrebody so hot gas always leaves sheathed in cool air.

{{FIG_MOD}}

Bypass ratio ≈ 100

The fan passes ~82 lb/s; the core passes ~0.7. That is not a turbofan — it is a turboshaft driving a very large, very slow fan, and that is exactly right, because hover power scales as 1/√(disc area). A conventional turbofan matched to the same 675 lbf would need a 10.8-inch fan, 1/23 the disc area and four times the power. The core is small and buried because the fan has to be big.

Why the turbine works here, when it didn't before

Earlier revisions rejected turbines on throttle lag: a gas turbine changes thrust in 1–2 seconds, and a four-rotor hover needs corrections at 10–50 Hz. This architecture answers it twice over. Spool before commitment — the cores reach stable flight idle while the vehicle is still standing on its tires, pointing thrust somewhere harmless, so nothing has to accelerate on demand. And vector, don't fuel — lift is commanded by roll angle and blade pitch, both fast and mechanical, with variable pitch responding in ~30 ms. Constant-speed core plus variable pitch is how every turbine-lift aircraft has ever been controlled.

The throttle-lag objection is withdrawn. The objections that survive are mass, four engines, and engine-out — and those are real (§07).

Deleting the hull fan

Deleted. Each wheel now carries 675 lbf instead of 560, which is within the same module. In exchange: the centreline is freed for crew, fuel and battery; the "mandatory fan" failure case disappears; the hull becomes pure structure with nothing spinning under the floor; and the vehicle gets 1.6 feet shorter. There was no argument for keeping it.

Human factors

  • Ingress over a 16-inch sill, stepping on the rail housing — a deliberate foothold, flat and gritted, not an accident of packaging.
  • Seating side by side, 28-inch hip spacing, reclined 18° for a low canopy.
  • Sight lines: the nose drops away between the front wheels, so the ground is visible 12 feet ahead — set by the landing case, not the driving case.
  • Hot-surface discipline: the spinner and exhaust path are the only touch hazards, and both are outboard, orange and thermochromic.
  • Noise: ~95–100 dBA at 50 ft in hover. Crew on intercom and ANR. The vehicle is not quiet, and doctrine is the mitigation — fly briefly, land out of sight, approach on wheels and on battery.

The modules are half the vehicle. That is the defining consequence of putting engines in the wheels, and §07 prices it.

{{FIG_PWR}}

Hover sets installed power; cruise sets fuel. Hovering 60 seconds costs 8 pounds of Jet-A — nothing. What hover costs is the 780 hp installation carried through every minute of cruise. Judge changes on installed horsepower.

And the vehicle is drag-limited, not power-limited. Four 52-inch ducts in the airstream dominate: unfaired, top speed falls to ~130 mph; faired, 152. Duct fairing is worth more than any engine change on the table, and it is cheap.

07What it costs — and the one unsolved problem

Mass. Turbine-in-wheel makes the vehicle roughly 50% heavier than the electric equivalent at the same mission — 2,700 lb against ~1,830. Four cores and four gearboxes weigh more than one core, a generator and four motors, and the wheel has to carry its own hot section, oil and fuel line. Bought with that mass: no high-voltage flight bus, no generator chain and its 8–10% loss, fuel energy delivered at the thrust source, and the spool-before-commitment behaviour that makes the whole architecture work.

Four engines to start, synchronise, maintain and overhaul, with fuel and hot gas crossing two moving joints per corner.

Engine-out in hover is the open problem, and it is not solved. Lose one core with the discs flat and the vehicle loses 25% of lift and gains a large asymmetric moment. Four independent cores cannot cross-feed the way four electric motors can.

  • Rim-motor boost. The ground-drive motor is rated for a 60-second emergency pull of ~30 hp — enough to turn an immediate roll departure into a controllable descent, not enough to hold hover. Honest about that.
  • Fly the corridor fast. Exposure is seconds, not minutes, by design.
  • Ballistic recovery for the crewed variant, sized for the transformation envelope.

If this cannot be closed in Gen-1 testing, the honest fallback is a single core in the body driving tip-turbine fans in the rims — Ryan XV-5 precedent, with a Harrier-style hot-gas swivel at the roll joint. One engine, inherently cross-fed, at the cost of ducting and noise. That is the fork to watch.

08Make, maintain, and what's next

  • Four identical modules, no handedness — mirrored mounting only.
  • Module change: six bolts, one fuel quick-disconnect, one electrical connector. Target 12 minutes, two people, no lift equipment — the module rolls away on its own tire.
  • Hull is three mouldings plus two rail extrusions; the rails are primary structure and the skins are not.
  • The core is a bought-in gas generator, not a bespoke engine. The programme does not develop a turbine; it packages one.

Roadmap

  • Gen-0 — one module on a stand. Core, gearbox, variable pitch, roll and slide under load. Prove 120 hp ideal at FoM ≥ 0.6 and the 20-second spool.
  • Gen-0.5 — transformation rig. No flight. A ballasted chassis that performs all four beats, ten thousand cycles, in mud and grit. The transformation is the product; de-risk it before anything flies.
  • Gen-1 — tethered hover, then free transitions, then the corridor.
  • Gen-2 — environmental and engine-out: the §07 gate.
  • Gen-3 — range kit: the 16-foot deployable span.

The unmanned sibling

The same kinematics scale down; the turbine does not. Below roughly 100 hp, small-core thermal efficiency and tip-clearance losses make gas turbines worse than batteries on every axis. The sub-100 lb scout therefore keeps the four-beat transformation, the slide rail, the hubless rim and the flank channel, and replaces each core with a coaxial electric fan pair. The architecture is the product; the powerplant is scale-dependent.
