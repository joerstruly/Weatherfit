RW-5 Ferret Rotorwheel

A hybrid ground–air scout: four small hubless wheels, each holding a pair of contra-rotating ducted fans, that flip flat to fly and then pitch forward to fly fast; a lift fan buried in a delta lifting-body hull; an F1 nose narrow enough for doorways. Ground for endurance, air for obstacles and speed.

01Concept & mission profile

Wheel-sized rotors are aerodynamically expensive — §06 puts numbers on it — so hover is a transition state, not a loiter mode. The airframe is built to get through hover and into cruise, where pitched discs and the lifting hull make flight roughly half as costly. On the ground it is a skid-steered scout that fits through a doorway; in the air it is a fast repositioning vehicle that hops obstacles, transfers between floors and rooftops, and dashes to the next vantage.

02Hull — delta lifting body

Planform: an F1-style nose 160 mm wide at the sensor tip, sitting between the front wheels, widening in a straight delta to a 440 mm hull — 680 mm overall — at the rear wheels, with a blunt Kamm tail. Section: a cambered thick airfoil front to back: low nose, maximum thickness of ~200 mm ahead of mid-chord, tapering to a 70 mm trailing edge over a 1,200 mm chord.

The numbers are modest and worth stating plainly: planform ≈ 0.25 m² at an effective aspect ratio around 0.7. At 110 km/h and ~8° angle of attack (CL ≈ 0.7) the hull carries about 26% of the vehicle's weight; at 145 km/h about 46%. It never carries all of it — the hull's job is to unload the rotors, not to glide. On the ground the same shape gives a low sloped signature and a flat belly for skid landings.

03Rotor-wheel module

A hubless Ø 400 mm wheel whose open center holds two contra-rotating Ø 320 mm fans, 55 mm apart, each on its own tip-drive ring motor. Outside them, from the tire inward: a 130 mm airless shear-band tire, an in-rim ground drive motor (1.2 kW continuous, no gearbox), and the structural rim that doubles as the 100 mm deep duct wall. Debris mesh covers both faces. The hub is static — no shaft passes through the center, which is what makes the two-stage stack possible in a wheel this small.

Why coaxial

  • Torque-neutral per wheel. The stages cancel each other's reaction torque, so there is no CW/CCW pairing across the vehicle and no yaw upset when a wheel fails.
  • ~35% more thrust from the same Ø 320 disc than a single stage, at roughly 1.7× the power. This is the trade that lets the wheel shrink from Ø 520 to Ø 400.
  • Graceful degradation: one dead stage leaves ~60% of that wheel's thrust.
  • The cost: ~10% worse figure of merit than a single rotor of the same area, plus a second motor, mesh and controller per wheel.

Why two-axis tilt

Each mount first flips the wheel 95° about the fore-aft axis (vertical → horizontal, outboard), then pitches it from −20° to +70° about the lateral axis. Coaxial units produce no reaction torque, so yaw has to come from vectoring: differential fore/aft pitch — left discs forward, right discs back — yaws the vehicle in hover, and symmetric forward pitch is the cruise propulsion. The same authority trims the lifting body across the speed range. Both axes lock mechanically at their endpoints, so neither mode needs power to hold.

Staggered discs — rear pair 300 mm higher

Coplanar, the rear discs would fly in the front discs' wake in cruise: 10–15% thrust loss, vibration, noise — the reason the CH-47's rear pylon stands taller than its front. Over the 720 mm wheelbase at 110 km/h the front wake descends ~0.2 m under its own induced velocity but rises ~0.1 m in the body frame because the hull flies nose-up, so it reaches the rear axle only ~0.1 m below the front disc plane with a radius of at least 0.16 m. Clearing it needs the rear disc centre 0.22 m above the front; with margin, 300 mm, 0.75 disc diameters. The front flip pivots sit low on the flank and the rear pivots on raised shoulder pylons with longer swing-arms, so the stagger appears only when the wheels flip; in drive mode all four sit at axle height. Cruise AoA is held at 5° rather than 8°, since every degree of nose-up pushes the wake toward the rear discs. Two couplings go into the control law: a pitch moment from the vertical offset of the thrust lines during translation, and a nose-down tendency as the higher rear discs leave ground effect first at liftoff.

Failure isolation: the ground and flight drivetrains share nothing but the rim. A wheel with dead fans still drives; a wheel with a dead rim motor still lifts. Drive mode works with any flight subsystem dead, and land-and-drive is the universal abort.

04Embedded lift fan & vane box

A Ø 380 mm seven-blade fan sits fixed and vertical in the rear hull at ~62% chord, between the rear wheels, fed by a top inlet with two doors that close in drive mode so the wing surface stays clean and the fan stays protected. Beneath it, a cascade of pivoting vanes — a vane box — deflects the exhaust from straight down to 50° aft.

This is how the rear fan "tilts forward": the fan does not move; its thrust does. A Ø 380 fan physically tilting inside a 200 mm thick hull needs a cutout that removes most of the rear planform and the wing's upper surface; vanes cost ~4% of thrust and no structure. The precedent is the F-35B lift-fan vane box. The fan carries ~25% of hover thrust and gives direct pitch trim at liftoff, supplies forward thrust through the transition, and unloads toward pure propulsion in cruise.

06The hard problems

An honest concept spec earns its keep here. Rev B fixed Rev A's tail-rotor and cruise problems and bought new ones.

6.1 — Hover got worse, cruise got better

Smaller wheels cut disc area by a third; the coaxial stages recover the thrust but not the efficiency, so hover now costs 11.6 kW for 38 kg (Rev A: 11 kW for 42 kg). The lifting body and the pitching discs are the counterweight: cruise at ~6.5 kW. Net effect — hover is a 9-minute transition budget and useful flight is forward flight. Do not design missions around hovering.

6.2 — Complexity is now the dominant risk

13 motors, 9 actuators, 8 mesh faces, 4 two-axis mounts. Each rotor-wheel is a small tilt-rotor nacelle that also has to survive being a wheel. Mitigations: module-level replaceability (one connector, four bolts), drive mode fully functional with any flight subsystem dead, land-and-drive as the abort.

6.3 — A low-aspect-ratio hull is not a wing

At AR ≈ 0.7 the lift slope is shallow and induced drag is heavy; it will not glide. The hull earns its keep only above ~90 km/h. Below that it is a fairing, and in hover it is a flat plate sitting in the downwash of four discs — expect 4–6% download on the outboard hull edges.

6.4 — The vane box lives where the mud is

The vane cascade is under the vehicle, exactly where mud packs. Vanes park closed behind a belly door in drive mode; after deep mud, VTOL is degraded until a spin-clean cycle or manual clearing.

6.5 — The lift fan is mandatory, not a helper

The lift fan sits at ~62% chord, aft of the CG (~45%). That is deliberate — the vane box and disc pitch give strong pitch authority — but it means losing the lift fan in hover leaves the four discs at ~75% of required thrust: the vehicle must land within seconds, descending. Rev A's tail fan was optional; Rev B's is not. Fan-out redundancy on the lift fan is a Gen-1 design item.

07Scaling to people

The architecture scales; the geometry does not. Rotor area is pinned to wheel size and grows with L² while mass grows with L³, so disc loading rises linearly with scale and hover power per kilogram with its square root. A 4-seat version of this configuration would need ~900 kW to hover — five times an R44 of the same mass — and is not worth building. A 2-seat version is defensible, provided the wheels stop being the primary lift.

08RW-2S — two-seat variant

Body-lift-dominant: two Ø 1.7 m fans buried in the hull fore and aft of a side-by-side cabin carry ~75% of hover thrust; the four Ø 0.82 m coaxial rotor-wheels carry the rest and provide ground drive, hover yaw and cruise propulsion. A ~300 kW turboshaft generator in the tail feeds every motor, with a 15 kWh battery for hover peak-shaving and a silent electric ground mode. The rear discs sit 0.5 m above the front pair on shoulder pylons.

What changes at this scale

  • The doorway advantage is gone. At 2.6 m wide it does not go inside buildings. The mission becomes rough-terrain mobility plus short-hop VTOL for a two-person team — where a helicopter can't land and a truck can't drive. Precedent: DARPA Transformer TX / Advanced Tactics Black Knight, 2014.
  • Hover is turbine-fuelled. 300 kW for 10 minutes is 50 kWh — 200 kg of cells, or ~22 kg of Jet-A. The battery's job is peak-shaving and the silent electric ground mode, which is the tactical feature worth having.
  • Packaging drives the length. Two Ø 1.7 fans, a 1.2 m cabin and a turbine in the tail is 6.2 m. A single Ø 2.2 fan behind the cabin would be shorter but costs ~8% hover power and makes the aft fan even more mandatory.
  • Three times an R22's power for the same seats. That is the price of keeping rotors in wheels. RW-2S is defensible only where the ground mode does most of the work.

09Turbines at human scale

"Small turbines instead of the dual fans" is three different ideas, and only one is good.

OptionWhat it isVerdict
A. Micro-turbojets in the wheelsReplace each coaxial fan pair with a ~600 N turbojet (JetCat P550 class)Reject. Turbojet fuel burn is ~150 kg per kN·h: 8 kN of hover thrust burns ~10 kg of fuel a minute — jet-suit endurance, 5–8 min. Exhaust at 600 °C and ~400 m/s inside a tire destroys the tire, the ground and anyone nearby. FOD ingestion at 100,000 rpm turns every mud and debris problem into a blade-out. Thermal soak rules out drive mode after flight. And turbojets are propulsively inefficient below ~300 km/h — the wrong engine for a 160 km/h vehicle.
B. Small turbofans as the hull lift fansA gas-turbine core driving each Ø 1.7 fan mechanicallyPossible, second choice. Efficient enough, fans stay cold and mesh-protected. But gearboxes and shafting through the hull, two hot cores under the cabin, no silent electric mode, and the redundancy of independent electric motors is lost.
C. Turboshaft generator, electric fans everywhereOne turbine in the tail turning a generator; every fan and wheel stays electricRecommended. Fuel at ~3.5 kWh/kg after a 30% turbine is 14× battery — the one thing that lets an 800 kg VTOL hover for more than a few minutes. The turbine sits protected in the tail with clean inlet air and a Kamm-tail exhaust. Every rotor stays electric: fine control, redundancy, mesh protection, silent battery driving. Small turboshafts burn ~0.45 kg/kWh, which is why fuel is 80 kg — still a tenth the mass of the equivalent battery.

Rule of thumb: turbines belong in the body as the power source, never in the wheels as thrusters. The point of a rotor-wheel is a fan you can drive on; a turbine is a fan you cannot get near.

10Development path

  • Gen-0, bench — one coaxial rotor-wheel on a thrust stand and dyno: validate FoM ≥ 0.55 with mesh, both stages' thermals, two-axis mount loads.
  • Gen-0.3, tunnel — a 1:2 lifting-body model: confirm CL ≈ 0.7 at 8° and pitch behaviour with the lift-fan inlet open and closed.
  • Gen-0.5, surrogate — a COTS tilt-rotor quad on a skid-steer chassis, flown against the mission profile — proves the doctrine before paying for integration.
  • Gen-1 — full airframe: tethered hover, free transitions, hover → cruise.
  • Gen-2 — environmental hardening (mud, sand, EMI) and autonomous doorway / stairwell traversal.
