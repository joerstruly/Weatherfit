RW-5 Ferret Rotorwheel

A hybrid ground–air scout: four small hubless wheels, each holding a pair of contra-rotating ducted fans, that flip flat to fly and then pitch forward to fly fast; a lift fan buried in a delta lifting-body hull; an F1 nose narrow enough for doorways. Ground for endurance, air for obstacles and speed.

Rev D: identical wheel arms front and rear — the rear pair clears the front wake because the vehicle cruises nose-down, not because of geometry; RW-2S re-laid-out around four Ø 1.4 m rotor-wheels with no fans in the hull; turbine-in-the-wheel assessed as a turbofan matching problem and rejected (§09). Rev C had added the stagger, RW-2S (§08) and turbines (§09). Rev B superseded Rev A: coaxial two-stage fans in Ø 400 wheels (was single-stage in Ø 520), two-axis tilt (flip + fore/aft pitch), lift fan embedded in the hull behind a thrust-vectoring vane box (was a tail pylon), and a delta lifting-body hull.

01Concept & mission profile

Wheel-sized rotors are aerodynamically expensive — §06 puts numbers on it — so hover is a transition state, not a loiter mode. The airframe is built to get through hover and into cruise, where pitched discs and the lifting hull make flight roughly half as costly. On the ground it is a skid-steered scout that fits through a doorway; in the air it is a fast repositioning vehicle that hops obstacles, transfers between floors and rooftops, and dashes to the next vantage.

02Hull — delta lifting body

Planform: an F1-style nose 160 mm wide at the sensor tip, sitting between the front wheels, widening in a straight delta to a 440 mm hull — 680 mm overall — at the rear wheels, with a blunt Kamm tail. Section: a cambered thick airfoil front to back: low nose, maximum thickness of ~200 mm ahead of mid-chord, tapering to a 70 mm trailing edge over a 1,200 mm chord.

The numbers are modest and worth stating plainly: planform ≈ 0.25 m² at an effective aspect ratio around 0.7. Level at +5° and 110 km/h (CL ≈ 0.7) the hull would carry about 26% of the vehicle's weight. But Rev D cruises 15° nose-down for wake clearance (§03), so the hull lifts only in level transition; in cruise it is a fairing that makes some downforce. On the ground the same shape gives a low sloped signature and a flat belly for skid landings.

03Rotor-wheel module

A hubless Ø 400 mm wheel whose open center holds two contra-rotating Ø 320 mm fans, 55 mm apart, each on its own tip-drive ring motor. Outside them, from the tire inward: a 130 mm airless shear-band tire, an in-rim ground drive motor (1.2 kW continuous, no gearbox), and the structural rim that doubles as the 100 mm deep duct wall. Debris mesh covers both faces. The hub is static — no shaft passes through the center, which is what makes the two-stage stack possible in a wheel this small.

Why coaxial

  • Torque-neutral per wheel. The stages cancel each other's reaction torque, so there is no CW/CCW pairing across the vehicle and no yaw upset when a wheel fails.
  • ~35% more thrust from the same Ø 320 disc than a single stage, at roughly 1.7× the power. This is the trade that lets the wheel shrink from Ø 520 to Ø 400.
  • Graceful degradation: one dead stage leaves ~60% of that wheel's thrust.
  • The cost: ~10% worse figure of merit than a single rotor of the same area, plus a second motor, mesh and controller per wheel.

Why two-axis tilt

Each mount first flips the wheel 95° about the fore-aft axis (vertical → horizontal, outboard), then pitches it from −20° to +70° about the lateral axis. Coaxial units produce no reaction torque, so yaw has to come from vectoring: differential fore/aft pitch — left discs forward, right discs back — yaws the vehicle in hover, and symmetric forward pitch is the cruise propulsion. The same authority trims the lifting body across the speed range. Both axes lock mechanically at their endpoints, so neither mode needs power to hold.

Stagger by attitude, not by arms

Coplanar and level, the rear discs would fly in the front discs' wake in cruise: 10–15% thrust loss, vibration, noise. Rev C fixed that with raised rear pivots and longer rear arms. Rev D drops it: all four arms and pivots are identical, and the whole vehicle cruises nose-down, so the rear pair rides higher in the airflow the way a multirotor's does. At 15° nose-down the attitude alone lifts the rear discs 720 mm × sin 15° = 0.19 m relative to the airflow, and the front wake also descends ~0.2 m over the wheelbase under its own induced velocity, so it passes ~0.4 m under the rear pair. The discs pitch 45° to the hull, which puts their thrust 60° to the air — the same as before.

What it costs: a lifting body at −15° is a brake. The cambered hull that carried ~26% of the weight at +5° now makes ~19% of the weight in downforce and roughly triple the drag. Cruise power rises from ≈ 6.5 to ≈ 8.5 kW; endurance falls from ~17 to ~13 min, range from ~30 to ~24 km. The trade buys four fewer bespoke parts, no pylons, and a rear pair in genuinely clean air. If the wing matters more, the alternative is a hull built with +12° incidence to the wheel plane — it would sit nose-high on the ground, and was not adopted.

Failure isolation: the ground and flight drivetrains share nothing but the rim. A wheel with dead fans still drives; a wheel with a dead rim motor still lifts. Drive mode works with any flight subsystem dead, and land-and-drive is the universal abort.

04Embedded lift fan & vane box

A Ø 380 mm seven-blade fan sits fixed and vertical in the rear hull at ~62% chord, between the rear wheels, fed by a top inlet with two doors that close in drive mode so the wing surface stays clean and the fan stays protected. Beneath it, a cascade of pivoting vanes — a vane box — deflects the exhaust from straight down to 50° aft.

This is how the rear fan "tilts forward": the fan does not move; its thrust does. A Ø 380 fan physically tilting inside a 200 mm thick hull needs a cutout that removes most of the rear planform and the wing's upper surface; vanes cost ~4% of thrust and no structure. The precedent is the F-35B lift-fan vane box. The fan carries ~25% of hover thrust and gives direct pitch trim at liftoff, supplies forward thrust through the transition, and unloads toward pure propulsion in cruise.

06The hard problems

An honest concept spec earns its keep here. Rev B fixed Rev A's tail-rotor and cruise problems and bought new ones.

6.1 — Hover got worse, cruise got better

Smaller wheels cut disc area by a third; the coaxial stages recover the thrust but not the efficiency, so hover now costs 11.6 kW for 38 kg (Rev A: 11 kW for 42 kg). The lifting body and the pitching discs are the counterweight: cruise at ~6.5 kW. Net effect — hover is a 9-minute transition budget and useful flight is forward flight. Do not design missions around hovering.

6.2 — Complexity is now the dominant risk

13 motors, 9 actuators, 8 mesh faces, 4 two-axis mounts. Each rotor-wheel is a small tilt-rotor nacelle that also has to survive being a wheel. Mitigations: module-level replaceability (one connector, four bolts), drive mode fully functional with any flight subsystem dead, land-and-drive as the abort.

6.3 — A low-aspect-ratio hull is not a wing

At AR ≈ 0.7 the lift slope is shallow and induced drag is heavy; it will not glide. The hull earns its keep only above ~90 km/h. Below that it is a fairing, and in hover it is a flat plate sitting in the downwash of four discs — expect 4–6% download on the outboard hull edges.

6.4 — The vane box lives where the mud is

The vane cascade is under the vehicle, exactly where mud packs. Vanes park closed behind a belly door in drive mode; after deep mud, VTOL is degraded until a spin-clean cycle or manual clearing.

6.5 — The lift fan is mandatory, not a helper

The lift fan sits at ~62% chord, aft of the CG (~45%). That is deliberate — the vane box and disc pitch give strong pitch authority — but it means losing the lift fan in hover leaves the four discs at ~75% of required thrust: the vehicle must land within seconds, descending. Rev A's tail fan was optional; Rev B's is not. Fan-out redundancy on the lift fan is a Gen-1 design item.

07Scaling to people

The architecture scales; the geometry does not. Rotor area is pinned to wheel size and grows with L² while mass grows with L³, so disc loading rises linearly with scale and hover power per kilogram with its square root. A 4-seat version of this configuration would need ~900 kW to hover — five times an R44 of the same mass — and is not worth building. A 2-seat version is defensible, provided the wheels stop being the primary lift.

08RW-2S — two-seat variant

Four large rotor-wheels carry all the lift; there are no fans in the hull. Wheel size is the variable that makes this work: at Ø 1.4 m the four coaxial Ø 1.2 m discs give 4.5 m² — as much as Rev C's two hull fans and four small wheels combined — in a hull 1.6 m shorter. The wheels are ~30% of vehicle length, the same proportion as RW-5. Arms and pivots are identical front and rear; the rear pair clears the front wake because the vehicle cruises 12° nose-down. A ~350 kW turboshaft generator and the fuel sit in the tail, the battery in the nose, the crew in the middle, and nothing under the cabin floor spins.

What changes at this scale

  • Bigger wheels are right, aerodynamically and on the ground. Every centimetre of wheel diameter is disc area, and Ø 1.4 rolls 0.45 m steps. The cost is module mass: 55 kg a corner, half the empty weight.
  • No hull fans means the hull is just a hull. It also removes the mandatory-fan failure case: lose one wheel stage and the other three-and-a-half discs still hover it.
  • The doorway advantage is gone at 2.2 m wide; the mission is rough-terrain mobility plus short-hop VTOL for a two-person team.
  • Hover is turbine-fuelled. 350 kW for 10 minutes is 58 kWh — 230 kg of cells or ~26 kg of Jet-A. The battery's job is peak-shaving and the silent electric ground mode.
  • Still ~3.5× an R22's power for the same seats — the price of rotors sized to wheels rather than wheels sized to rotors.

09Turbines at human scale

The proposal is small gas turbines in the wheels — turbofans, as on an airliner, scaled down — instead of electric fans. The answer turns on a matching problem, so it is worth working properly rather than asserting.

A turbofan is a gas core driving a ducted fan

That is the whole architecture: a combustion core extracts shaft power and spins a ducted fan, and on a high-bypass engine that fan makes 80–90% of the thrust while the core exhaust is nearly an afterthought. The rotor-wheel already has the ducted fan. So a turbofan in the wheel adds no new kind of thruster — it moves the combustor from the tail into the wheel and swaps the electric ring motor for a gas core. The real question is narrow: what spins the fan, and where does the fuel burn?

The matching problem

One RW-2S wheel must make ~2.0 kN in hover. There are two ways to make that thrust, and they are not close:

Fan diameterØ 1.20 m — fills the Ø 1.4 m wheelØ 0.23 m (BPR 8, FPR 1.4)
Disc area1.13 m²0.041 m² — 1/28 as much
Disc loading180 kg/m²~5,000 kg/m²
Jet velocity54 m/s, ambient240 m/s, 300 °C+
Ideal hover power55 kW290 kW

Hover power goes as T1.5/√(2ρA). Thrust is fixed, so power scales with 1/√(disc area), and √28 ≈ 5.3: the same lift costs five times the power. In fuel — after crediting the small core's ~22% thermal efficiency against the turboshaft's ~19% — that is roughly 3–4× the burn, about 6–8 kg/min in hover against 2.6.

This is not a detail better design fixes. Propulsive efficiency is 2 / (1 + vjet/vflight); in hover vflight = 0, so every jet sits at zero propulsive efficiency and the only lever left is to move a lot of air slowly. A turbofan's entire design intent is the opposite — move less air faster — because at 900 km/h that is the efficient choice.

The bypass ratio proves it

Turn the question around: keep the Ø 1.2 m fan and ask what core would drive it. The fan passes ~37 kg/s; a core making the required ~55 kW of shaft power passes about 0.3 kg/s. That is a bypass ratio near 120. Airliner turbofans run 5–12, the GE9X is 10, and open-rotor demonstrators reach 30–40 — and those are already geared turboshafts driving propellers, not turbofans. Nothing at BPR 120 is a turbofan. It is a turboshaft driving a fan, which is precisely the recommended architecture; the only thing still in question is where the combustor sits.

Core in the wheel, or core in the tail?

With the fan settled, the choice is mechanical drive in each wheel against electric drive from one turbine in the tail. Two honest points for the wheel: direct drive skips the generator–inverter–motor chain and its ~8–10% loss, and mass is close to a wash — one 350 kW turboshaft with generator, inverters and four motors is ~160 kg; four ~90 kW turboshafts with four reduction gearboxes is also ~160 kg. Against it, in order of severity:

  • Throttle lag. A gas turbine changes thrust in 1–2 s as its spool accelerates; an electric fan does it in ~50 ms, and a four-rotor hover is held together by corrections at 10–50 Hz. Turbine-driven lift has never been controlled by throttling the turbine — the Harrier and F-35B use bleed-air reaction jets and fast vanes, helicopters use collective pitch on a constant-speed rotor. A turbine-in-wheel would need variable-pitch coaxial fans on constant-speed cores: heavier and more complex than the electric stages it replaces.
  • Fuel and hot gas across a two-axis tilt joint, four times, on modules that are also wheels driving through mud. Electric modules need one cable.
  • A combustor inside the wheel that lands — hot section, oil system and FOD path in the one component guaranteed to meet debris and water.
  • Four engines to start, synchronise and maintain, instead of one.
  • No silent mode. Battery-electric driving with the turbine shut down is the vehicle's best tactical feature; turbines in the wheels delete it.
  • Thermal soak. Driving straight after flight puts a heat-soaked hot section beside the tire and the ground.

Conclusion — and where it doesn't apply

One turboshaft in the tail turning a generator; every fan stays electric. Fuel carries ~14× a battery's energy per kilogram, which is the only reason an 830 kg VTOL flies for more than a few minutes; the turbine sits protected with clean inlet air; and the rotors keep millisecond control, redundancy, mesh protection and a silent battery mode.

Cruise doesn't rescue the turbofan either. A turbofan wants vflight ≈ 0.5–0.7 × vjet; at RW-2S's 150 km/h (42 m/s) the matched jet velocity is 60–85 m/s, which describes a ducted fan. There is no point in this vehicle's envelope where a turbofan is the right device.

Two footnotes for completeness. Combustion has been put in the rotor before: tip-jet rotorcraft — the Fairey Rotodyne, the Hughes XH-17 — piped hot gas to the blade tips, eliminating reaction torque and the transmission entirely, and were so loud and thirsty that none entered service. And if the airframe were far larger and hover were a negligible slice of the mission, jet lift starts to pay: that vehicle is the F-35B, and it is not this one.

Rule of thumb: turbines belong in the body as the power source, never in the wheels as thrusters.

10Development path

  • Gen-0, bench — one coaxial rotor-wheel on a thrust stand and dyno: validate FoM ≥ 0.55 with mesh, both stages' thermals, two-axis mount loads.
  • Gen-0.3, tunnel — a 1:2 lifting-body model: confirm CL ≈ 0.7 at 8° and pitch behaviour with the lift-fan inlet open and closed.
  • Gen-0.5, surrogate — a COTS tilt-rotor quad on a skid-steer chassis, flown against the mission profile — proves the doctrine before paying for integration.
  • Gen-1 — full airframe: tethered hover, free transitions, hover → cruise.
  • Gen-2 — environmental hardening (mud, sand, EMI) and autonomous doorway / stairwell traversal.
