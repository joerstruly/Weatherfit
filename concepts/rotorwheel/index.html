RW-5 Ferret Rotorwheel

Four hubless wheels, each holding a pair of contra-rotating ducted fans, that flip flat to fly and pitch forward to fly fast; a lift fan buried in a delta hull; an F1 nose narrow enough for doorways. Not a hovering machine that also travels — a travelling machine that can take off and land anywhere.

Rev E re-derives the vehicle around a point-to-point speed design point: hover is a 30-second transition at each end, not a loiter mode. The nose-down cruise attitude is withdrawn — the wake problem was overstated; fans become variable-pitch, without which there is no cruise; and span, not hull shape, turns out to be the dominant cruise-power lever. Rev D made the arms identical and re-laid-out RW-2S; Rev C added the stagger, RW-2S (§08) and turbines (§09). Rev B superseded Rev A: coaxial two-stage fans in Ø 15.7 in wheels (was single-stage in Ø 20.5 in), two-axis tilt (flip + fore/aft pitch), lift fan embedded in the hull behind a thrust-vectoring vane box (was a tail pylon), and a delta lifting-body hull.

Units: US customary throughout — inches and feet, pounds and pounds-force, mph, horsepower, lb/ft² of disc loading, lb/hp·h of fuel consumption. Battery capacity stays in kWh, the industry convention. The analysis was performed in SI and converted; figures are rounded to the precision the estimate supports.

01Design point — point-to-point speed

Hover is a transition of roughly 30 seconds at each end of a sortie, not a mission phase. That single statement reorders every trade in this document, and it is worth being blunt about what it overturns: earlier revisions weighted hover efficiency heavily, and for this design point that was the wrong emphasis.

  • Hover sets installed power. Cruise sets energy. Two budgets, easily confused. At RW-2S scale hover needs ~470 hp but runs ~60 s per sortie — 5.8 kWh, a third of the battery, negligible as fuel. Cruise runs 30–60 minutes and spends every pound aboard.
  • So disc loading is not a fuel penalty here — but it still sizes the powerplant. The vehicle carries a 470 hp installation to use 295–429 hp in cruise, and that mass is carried every minute of the flight. Judge propulsion proposals on installed horsepower, not hover burn (§09 re-tests turbines on exactly this basis).
  • The cruise curve is the design. Power required against airspeed, with the installed-power line drawn across it, gives top speed, best-range speed and range in one picture.

Two things to read off it. The 469 hp line is set by hover and never by cruise — where it crosses the power curve is the top speed, so any hover-power increase is paid for in speed. And the gap between the curves is the prize in §02: span, not shape.

02Hull — delta lifting body

Planform: an F1-style nose 6.3 in wide at the sensor tip, sitting between the front wheels, widening in a straight delta to a 17.3 in hull — 26.8 in overall — at the rear wheels, with a blunt Kamm tail. Section: a cambered thick airfoil front to back: low nose, maximum thickness of ~7.9 in ahead of mid-chord, tapering to a 2.8 in trailing edge over a 47.2 in chord.

The numbers are modest and worth stating plainly: planform ≈ 2.7 ft² at an effective aspect ratio around 0.7. Level at +5° and 68 mph (CL ≈ 0.7) the hull would carry about 26% of the vehicle's weight. But Rev D cruises 15° nose-down for wake clearance (§03), so the hull lifts only in level transition; in cruise it is a fairing that makes some downforce. On the ground the same shape gives a low sloped signature and a flat belly for skid landings.

Span, not shape, is the cruise-power lever

At speed the dominant cost is the induced power of holding the vehicle up, Pi ≈ T²/(2ρAv). Taking lift off the rotors cuts it as the square of the thrust removed, which makes offloading onto a wing the largest single lever in the cruise budget — bigger than drag cleanup, bigger than rotor efficiency. The catch is that a lifting body is a poor wing, and the arithmetic is unforgiving. At RW-2S scale, 1,830 lb at 109 mph:

Rotors carry everythingLifting body, AR ≈ 0.7Deployable wing, AR ≈ 4
Rotor thrust1,830 lbf~1,045 lbf~1,045 lbf
Rotor induced power~165 hp~54 hp~54 hp
Wing induced drag—~306 lbf~47 lbf
Drag power~154 hp~266 hp~172 hp
Total cruise power~319 hp~319 hp~225 hp

At aspect ratio 0.7 the lifting body is exactly break-even. The induced drag it makes carrying the load costs precisely what the rotors saved. The hull shape still earns its keep for low-speed transition, ground signature and packaging — but as a wing at cruise it is doing nothing. Only span changes the answer.

Recommendation for the speed variant: a 13 ft deployable wing folding to the 7.2 ft driving width. It moves RW-2S from 122 to 141 mph and from ~62 to ~88 mi of range on the same 176 lb of fuel — the highest-value item on the roadmap. It is not in the baseline here only because folding structure at that span is its own engineering programme.

03Rotor-wheel module

A hubless Ø 15.7 in wheel whose open center holds two contra-rotating Ø 12.6 in fans, 2.2 in apart, each on its own tip-drive ring motor. Outside them, from the tire inward: a 5.1 in airless shear-band tire, an in-rim ground drive motor (1.6 hp continuous, no gearbox), and the structural rim that doubles as the 3.9 in deep duct wall. Debris mesh covers both faces. The hub is static — no shaft passes through the center, which is what makes the two-stage stack possible in a wheel this small.

Why coaxial

  • Torque-neutral per wheel. The stages cancel each other's reaction torque, so there is no CW/CCW pairing across the vehicle and no yaw upset when a wheel fails.
  • ~35% more thrust from the same Ø 12.6 in disc than a single stage, at roughly 1.7× the power. This is the trade that lets the wheel shrink from Ø 20.5 in to Ø 15.7 in.
  • Graceful degradation: one dead stage leaves ~60% of that wheel's thrust.
  • The cost: ~10% worse figure of merit than a single rotor of the same area, plus a second motor, mesh and controller per wheel.

Why two-axis tilt

Each mount first flips the wheel 95° about the fore-aft axis (vertical → horizontal, outboard), then pitches it from −20° to +70° about the lateral axis. Coaxial units produce no reaction torque, so yaw has to come from vectoring: differential fore/aft pitch — left discs forward, right discs back — yaws the vehicle in hover, and symmetric forward pitch is the cruise propulsion. The same authority trims the lifting body across the speed range. Both axes lock mechanically at their endpoints, so neither mode needs power to hold.

The wake problem, corrected

Two revisions of this document treated the rear discs flying in the front discs' wake as a first-order problem — Rev C raised the rear pivots, Rev D pitched the whole vehicle 15° nose-down. Reworked properly, the concern was overstated and both fixes are withdrawn.

The wake doesn't hang in place: it is driven down at roughly twice the disc's induced velocity while it drifts aft at flight speed, so descent over the wheelbase is 2·vi·(wheelbase / vflight). At RW-2S cruise — vi ≈ 49 ft/s, wheelbase 9.2 ft, 109 mph — that is ~5.6 ft, against a rear disc of 2 ft radius. The wake passes far beneath, and the margin only closes above about 155 mph, past this airframe's top speed. Slower flight makes it better, not worse. RW-5 is the tighter case (28.3 in wheelbase, Ø 12.6 in discs) and still clears comfortably.

So arms and pivots stay identical, and the vehicle no longer cruises nose-down — the hull flies level to +5°, which is the only attitude at which it contributes lift at all. The nose-down trick cost roughly a third of RW-2S's cruise efficiency and bought nothing. What remains real is transition, at 12–37 mph, where the wake is skewed but not yet swept clear: a control-authority and fatigue item for the conversion corridor, not a reason to reshape the vehicle.

Variable pitch is now mandatory

Fixed-pitch fans were adequate while flight meant short hops. For a vehicle whose job is cruise they are disqualifying, and this is the most consequential change in Rev E. A blade set for hover sees an inflow angle of about 11° at 75% radius; in cruise the axial inflow rises and the same blade sees about 19°. Fixed pitch therefore arrives at cruise at negative incidence — the fan makes drag, not thrust. Raising rpm to compensate needs ~755 ft/s tip speed, which is loud and raises thrust exactly when less is wanted.

So both coaxial stages get collective pitch, roughly 12–30° at 75% radius. The costs are real: a pitch mechanism inside a hubless rim is the hardest packaging problem in the module, and it adds ~15% to module mass. Besides making cruise possible at all, it buys constant-rpm operation — thrust responding through blade angle in ~30 ms rather than through rotor inertia — windmilling on a failed drive, and a true feathered position for drive mode.

Control — where this beats a quadcopter

The reason this layout suits point-to-point work isn't efficiency; it is that it can vector thrust without changing attitude. A quadcopter must pitch its whole airframe to accelerate or brake, which costs time, couples into lift, and points its sensors at the ground. Four two-axis mounts instead give:

  • Acceleration and braking at constant attitude — pitch the discs, keep the hull, the crew and the sensor line level. Hard braking on arrival needs no nose-up flare.
  • Yaw without reaction torque. Coaxial stages are torque-neutral, so yaw comes from differential fore/aft disc pitch — direct, and decoupled from thrust.
  • Gust rejection decoupled from trim. Attitude holds while the discs absorb the disturbance — worth far more at 109 mph than in a hover.

The matching risk is the conversion corridor. Between ~25 and ~68 mph the discs are partly edgewise, the duct lips load asymmetrically and produce a nose-up pitching moment, and the hull is not yet at useful dynamic pressure. Ducted fans are notably worse here than open rotors — it is what limited the Bell X-22 and the Doak VZ-4. The corridor has to be flown quickly and mapped early: it is the first flight-test objective after tethered hover, ahead of any speed number.

Failure isolation: the ground and flight drivetrains share nothing but the rim. A wheel with dead fans still drives; a wheel with a dead rim motor still lifts. Drive mode works with any flight subsystem dead, and land-and-drive is the universal abort.

04Embedded lift fan & vane box

A Ø 15.0 in seven-blade fan sits fixed and vertical in the rear hull at ~62% chord, between the rear wheels, fed by a top inlet with two doors that close in drive mode so the wing surface stays clean and the fan stays protected. Beneath it, a cascade of pivoting vanes — a vane box — deflects the exhaust from straight down to 50° aft.

This is how the rear fan "tilts forward": the fan does not move; its thrust does. A Ø 15 in fan physically tilting inside an 8 in thick hull needs a cutout that removes most of the rear planform and the wing's upper surface; vanes cost ~4% of thrust and no structure. The precedent is the F-35B lift-fan vane box. The fan carries ~25% of hover thrust and gives direct pitch trim at liftoff, supplies forward thrust through the transition, and unloads toward pure propulsion in cruise.

06The hard problems

An honest concept spec earns its keep here. Rev B fixed Rev A's tail-rotor and cruise problems and bought new ones.

6.1 — Hover got worse, cruise got better

Smaller wheels cut disc area by a third; the coaxial stages recover the thrust but not the efficiency, so hover now costs 15.6 hp for 84 lb (Rev A: 15 hp for 93 lb). The lifting body and the pitching discs are the counterweight: cruise at ~8.7 hp. Net effect — hover is a 9-minute transition budget and useful flight is forward flight. Do not design missions around hovering.

6.2 — Complexity is now the dominant risk

13 motors, 9 actuators, 8 mesh faces, 4 two-axis mounts. Each rotor-wheel is a small tilt-rotor nacelle that also has to survive being a wheel. Mitigations: module-level replaceability (one connector, four bolts), drive mode fully functional with any flight subsystem dead, land-and-drive as the abort.

6.3 — A low-aspect-ratio hull is not a wing

At AR ≈ 0.7 the lift slope is shallow and induced drag is heavy; it will not glide. The hull earns its keep only above ~56 mph. Below that it is a fairing, and in hover it is a flat plate sitting in the downwash of four discs — expect 4–6% download on the outboard hull edges.

6.4 — The vane box lives where the mud is

The vane cascade is under the vehicle, exactly where mud packs. Vanes park closed behind a belly door in drive mode; after deep mud, VTOL is degraded until a spin-clean cycle or manual clearing.

6.5 — The lift fan is mandatory, not a helper

The lift fan sits at ~62% chord, aft of the CG (~45%). That is deliberate — the vane box and disc pitch give strong pitch authority — but it means losing the lift fan in hover leaves the four discs at ~75% of required thrust: the vehicle must land within seconds, descending. Rev A's tail fan was optional; Rev B's is not. Fan-out redundancy on the lift fan is a Gen-1 design item.

07Scaling to people

The architecture scales; the geometry does not. Rotor area is pinned to wheel size and grows with L² while mass grows with L³, so disc loading rises linearly with scale and hover power per kilogram with its square root. A 4-seat version of this configuration would need ~1,200 hp to hover — five times an R44 of the same mass — and is not worth building. A 2-seat version is defensible, provided the wheels stop being the primary lift.

08RW-2S — two-seat variant

Four large rotor-wheels carry all the lift; there are no fans in the hull. Wheel size is the variable that makes this work: at Ø 55 in the four coaxial Ø 47 in discs give 48.7 ft² — as much as Rev C's two hull fans and four small wheels combined — in a hull 5 ft shorter. The wheels are ~30% of vehicle length, the same proportion as RW-5. Arms and pivots are identical front and rear; the rear pair clears the front wake because the vehicle cruises 12° nose-down. A ~470 hp turboshaft generator and the fuel sit in the tail, the battery in the nose, the crew in the middle, and nothing under the cabin floor spins.

What changes at this scale

  • Bigger wheels are right, aerodynamically and on the ground. Every inch of wheel diameter is disc area, and Ø 55 in rolls 18 in steps. The cost is module mass: 121 lb a corner, half the empty weight.
  • No hull fans means the hull is just a hull. It also removes the mandatory-fan failure case: lose one wheel stage and the other three-and-a-half discs still hover it.
  • The doorway advantage is gone at 7.2 ft wide; the mission is rough-terrain mobility plus short-hop VTOL for a two-person team.
  • Hover is turbine-fuelled. 470 hp for 10 minutes is 58 kWh — 507 lb of cells or ~57 lb (8.5 gal) of Jet-A. The battery's job is peak-shaving and the silent electric ground mode.
  • Still ~3.5× an R22's power for the same seats — the price of rotors sized to wheels rather than wheels sized to rotors.

09Turbines at human scale

The proposal is small gas turbines in the wheels — turbofans, as on an airliner, scaled down — instead of electric fans. The answer turns on a matching problem, so it is worth working properly rather than asserting.

A turbofan is a gas core driving a ducted fan

That is the whole architecture: a combustion core extracts shaft power and spins a ducted fan, and on a high-bypass engine that fan makes 80–90% of the thrust while the core exhaust is nearly an afterthought. The rotor-wheel already has the ducted fan. So a turbofan in the wheel adds no new kind of thruster — it moves the combustor from the tail into the wheel and swaps the electric ring motor for a gas core. The real question is narrow: what spins the fan, and where does the fuel burn?

The matching problem

One RW-2S wheel must make ~450 lbf in hover. There are two ways to make that thrust, and they are not close:

Specified rotor-wheelTurbofan matched to 450 lbf
Fan diameterØ 47 in — fills the Ø 55 in wheelØ 9.1 in (BPR 8, FPR 1.4)
Disc area12.2 ft²0.44 ft² — 1/28 as much
Disc loading36.8 lb/ft²~1,024 lb/ft²
Jet velocity121 mph, ambient537 mph, 570 °F+
Ideal hover power74 hp389 hp

Hover power goes as T1.5/√(2ρA). Thrust is fixed, so power scales with 1/√(disc area), and √28 ≈ 5.3: the same lift costs five times the power. In fuel — after crediting the small core's ~22% thermal efficiency against the turboshaft's ~19% — that is roughly 3–4× the burn, about 13–18 lb/min in hover against 5.7.

This is not a detail better design fixes. Propulsive efficiency is 2 / (1 + vjet/vflight); in hover vflight = 0, so every jet sits at zero propulsive efficiency and the only lever left is to move a lot of air slowly. A turbofan's entire design intent is the opposite — move less air faster — because at 560 mph that is the efficient choice.

The bypass ratio proves it

Turn the question around: keep the Ø 47 in fan and ask what core would drive it. The fan passes ~82 lb/s; a core making the required ~74 hp of shaft power passes about 0.66 lb/s. That is a bypass ratio near 120. Airliner turbofans run 5–12, the GE9X is 10, and open-rotor demonstrators reach 30–40 — and those are already geared turboshafts driving propellers, not turbofans. Nothing at BPR 120 is a turbofan. It is a turboshaft driving a fan, which is precisely the recommended architecture; the only thing still in question is where the combustor sits.

Core in the wheel, or core in the tail?

With the fan settled, the choice is mechanical drive in each wheel against electric drive from one turbine in the tail. Two honest points for the wheel: direct drive skips the generator–inverter–motor chain and its ~8–10% loss, and mass is close to a wash — one 470 hp turboshaft with generator, inverters and four motors is ~353 lb; four ~120 hp turboshafts with four reduction gearboxes is also ~353 lb. Against it, in order of severity:

  • Throttle lag. A gas turbine changes thrust in 1–2 s as its spool accelerates; an electric fan does it in ~50 ms, and a four-rotor hover is held together by corrections at 10–50 Hz. Turbine-driven lift has never been controlled by throttling the turbine — the Harrier and F-35B use bleed-air reaction jets and fast vanes, helicopters use collective pitch on a constant-speed rotor. A turbine-in-wheel would need variable-pitch coaxial fans on constant-speed cores: heavier and more complex than the electric stages it replaces.
  • Fuel and hot gas across a two-axis tilt joint, four times, on modules that are also wheels driving through mud. Electric modules need one cable.
  • A combustor inside the wheel that lands — hot section, oil system and FOD path in the one component guaranteed to meet debris and water.
  • Four engines to start, synchronise and maintain, instead of one.
  • No silent mode. Battery-electric driving with the turbine shut down is the vehicle's best tactical feature; turbines in the wheels delete it.
  • Thermal soak. Driving straight after flight puts a heat-soaked hot section beside the tire and the ground.

Re-tested against the speed design point

The hover-fuel case above largely dissolves under §01: if hover lasts 60 seconds, burning 3–4× the fuel during it costs ~44 lb, not a mission. So the honest question becomes installed power — and that is where it fails. Five times the hover power means ~2,350 hp installed to lift an 1,830 lb vehicle, against 335–429 hp actually used in cruise: 2,000-plus horsepower of engine, its mass and its cooling, carried every minute to use a fifth of it. The turboshaft-electric layout installs 470 hp and cruises on 295–429 hp — one powerplant doing both jobs.

One objection retracted. Throttle lag no longer counts against it: §03 adds variable-pitch fans for cruise reasons anyway, and variable pitch on a constant-speed core is exactly how turbine-driven lift has always been controlled. What remains is fuel and hot gas across four two-axis tilt joints, four engines, a combustor inside a landing wheel, no silent mode — and the installed power above.

Conclusion — and where it doesn't apply

One turboshaft in the tail turning a generator; every fan stays electric. Fuel carries ~14× a battery's energy per pound, which is the only reason an 1,830 lb VTOL flies for more than a few minutes; the turbine sits protected with clean inlet air; and the rotors keep millisecond control, redundancy, mesh protection and a silent battery mode.

Cruise doesn't rescue the turbofan either. A turbofan wants vflight ≈ 0.5–0.7 × vjet; at RW-2S's 93 mph (137 ft/s) the matched jet velocity is 134–190 mph, which describes a ducted fan. There is no point in this vehicle's envelope where a turbofan is the right device.

Two footnotes for completeness. Combustion has been put in the rotor before: tip-jet rotorcraft — the Fairey Rotodyne, the Hughes XH-17 — piped hot gas to the blade tips, eliminating reaction torque and the transmission entirely, and were so loud and thirsty that none entered service. And if the airframe were far larger and hover were a negligible slice of the mission, jet lift starts to pay: that vehicle is the F-35B, and it is not this one.

Rule of thumb: turbines belong in the body as the power source, never in the wheels as thrusters.

10Development path

  • Gen-0, bench — one coaxial rotor-wheel on a thrust stand and dyno: validate FoM ≥ 0.55 with mesh, both stages' thermals, two-axis mount loads.
  • Gen-0.3, tunnel — a 1:2 lifting-body model: confirm CL ≈ 0.7 at 8° and pitch behaviour with the lift-fan inlet open and closed.
  • Gen-0.5, surrogate — a COTS tilt-rotor quad on a skid-steer chassis, flown against the mission profile — proves the doctrine before paying for integration.
  • Gen-1 — full airframe: tethered hover, free transitions, hover → cruise.
  • Gen-2 — environmental hardening (mud, sand, EMI) and autonomous doorway / stairwell traversal.
