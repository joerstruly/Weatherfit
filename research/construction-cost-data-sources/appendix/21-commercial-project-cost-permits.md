# Non-residential project cost data and permit valuation datasets

## Tooling caveat (read first)

**WebFetch was blocked by the network egress proxy for every domain I tried** (`data.cityofnewyork.us`, `data.cityofchicago.org`, `dev.socrata.com`, `catalog.data.gov`, `docs.datasf.org`), and direct `curl` returns `CONNECT tunnel failed, 403`. So I could not open a single dataset page or API metadata endpoint. Everything below rests on **WebSearch result summaries only**. My tags therefore mean:

- **VERIFIED-DETAIL** — the specific fact (field name, coverage, number) was stated verbatim in a search-engine summary of the named source page. Not confirmed by opening the page.
- **VERIFIED-URL** — the URL appeared in search results; existence is confirmed, contents are not.
- **UNVERIFIED** — inferred or asserted by a secondary/low-quality source.
- **NOT FOUND** — searched, nothing credible.

Every URL below appeared in a search result. I used ~44 of ~45 searches.

### A5. THE CRITICAL CAVEAT — is "valuation" a cost observation or a formula output?

This is the most consequential thing I found, and the answer is: **in many jurisdictions it is a formula output.**

| Finding | URL | Detail | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ICC Building Valuation Data (BVD)** | https://www.iccsafe.org/products-and-services/i-codes/code-development-process/building-valuation-data/ · example table: https://www.iccsafe.org/wp-content/uploads/BVD-BSJ-AUG2026.pdf | ICC publishes a Square Foot Construction Cost table by IBC occupancy group × construction type, **updated every 6 months for 20+ years**. Jurisdictions compute: **Permit Fee = Gross Area × Square Foot Construction Cost**. ## Not found / dead ends — read this section

1. **Census BPS has zero non-residential coverage.** Non-residential permit collection was discontinued in **1995**. Any plan that assumed BPS could validate office/retail/industrial is dead. (VERIFIED-DETAIL)
2. **Census VIP carries no square footage and no project identity.** It is a spending flow, national-only, monthly. It cannot validate $/SF. It *can* calibrate escalation and now has a standalone data-center series.
3. **No free national permit aggregator with valuation exists.** Shovels (~$599/mo), BuildZoom/Gryd, Dodge, ConstructConnect, Construction Monitor are all paid. buildingpermitdata.org is a BuildZoom marketing site, not a free dataset.
4. **Seattle permits have EstProjectCost but NO square footage.** Chicago permits have reported_cost but **no square footage column surfaced**. These are two of the largest, cleanest permit APIs and both fail the $/SF requirement on their own. Any "big-city permit panel" plan must confirm sqft per city before committing.
5. **No peer-reviewed study quantifying the declared-permit-valuation vs. actual-construction-cost gap was found.** The best evidence is a **county review reported in local news** (Pitkin County: $253/SF declared vs ~$500/SF actual) and a **newspaper's characterization of Bay Area practice** (ICC valuations = ⅓ to ⅔ of actual). Flyvbjerg's 28% underestimation finding is about public-project estimates vs. outturn and is **not** about permits — do not misattribute it.
6. **No city audit measuring valuation accuracy was found.** All five permit-related audits located (NYC Comptroller, Volusia, Sarasota, Garland, Portland) are about fee-collection controls and lost revenue, not valuation-vs-cost.
7. **Task C benchmark searches for industrial/warehouse and retail/grocery returned almost entirely SEO content-farm pages** (nationalsteelbuildingscorp.com, maxxbuilders.com, terrapincg.com, buildermuse.com, crowntv-us.com, eb3construction.com, bhumicalculator.com). Figures like "$115–185/SF tilt-up industrial," "grocery $215/SF," "big-box shell $48/SF" have **no traceable methodology and no sample** — several look like stale numbers recycled from old Chain Store Age surveys. **Do not admit any of these into the model.** The only credible non-vendor free item found in Task C is the Turner &amp; Townsend data-centre index and the Census data-center VIP series.
8. **Not searched (budget exhausted):** Atlanta, Charlotte, Houston, Columbus, San Antonio, Portland permit datasets; Iowa Board of Regents; SUNY Construction Fund; Texas A&amp;M / UT System board minutes; GSA IOLP inventory; utility interconnection queue filings; Ohio Facilities Construction Commission school cost data. These are **unsearched, not absent** — Ohio FCC and SUNY in particular are likely productive on the MSBA model.
9. **Phoenix's open-data "permit" file is HUD SOCDS aggregate output, not project records.** Project-level Phoenix data exists only behind a search UI with CSV export, no API.

## Verdict: is permit valuation usable as a non-residential validation set?

**Yes — but only as a censored, downward-biased lower bound, never as a cost observation, and only from the handful of cities that publish square footage alongside value.**

Three defects, in order of severity. First, **in a material and unknown share of jurisdictions the "valuation" is literally `Gross Area × ICC Building Valuation Data rate`, not a reported cost.** Portland states this in writing — valuation is the *greater of* the ICC formula output or the applicant's stated value — and Oregon mandates the method statewide via OAR 918-050-0100; San Diego, LA County, Contra Costa County, Bellingham, Concord and Hopkins all publish their own valuation schedules or adopt the ICC table outright. Where that method binds, regressing declared value on square footage recovers the ICC table, not the market. That is not a weak signal, it is a **circular** one, and it would silently validate the model against a fee schedule. Any use of permit valuation must first classify each jurisdiction as cost-of-work-declared vs. table-derived — UpCodes' collated code sections (https://up.codes/s/building-permit-valuations-based-on-cost-of-work) is the practical starting point — and **drop or separately flag the table-derived jurisdictions**. Second, where valuation *is* applicant-declared, it is systematically understated because it sets the fee: the one quantified public review I found (Pitkin County) put declared value at roughly **half** of actual, and a Bay Area account put ICC-based valuations at **one-third to two-thirds** of actual. Third, permit valuation covers a **different scope** than a feasibility cost model: it typically excludes land, soft costs, FF&amp;E, tenant improvements filed under separate permits, and often site work — and for a phased commercial project the total is split across a foundation permit, a shell permit and multiple TI permits that must be re-assembled by parcel or job number before any $/SF is meaningful.

Practically, the usable core is small: **Austin** (`total_job_valuation` + `total_new_add_sqft` + work/permit class, Socrata, free), **Boston** (`DECLARED_VALUATION` + `sq_feet` + `OCCUPANCYTYPE`, CKAN, free), and **NYC DOB** (`Initial Cost` + `Existing/Proposed Zoning Sqft` + `Existing Occupancy`, Socrata, free) are the three where I confirmed all three required fields in one table. Seattle and Chicago are out on square footage. Everything else needs field-level confirmation I could not perform because the proxy blocked every dataset page. For the non-residential validation set proper, **MSBA's Designer Construction Cost Chart and Historical Cost Data (project name + GSF + reconciled construction budget + total project cost + year, 2019–2023) and the GSA prospectus library (ETPC + GSF per project, FY-filterable) are strictly better evidence than any permit file** — they are actual owner budgets at a defined design stage, and they should carry more weight in calibration even though they are institutional and federal rather than speculative commercial.

**The caveat that must be published alongside any permit-valuation figure in the catalog:** *"Declared permit valuation is a fee basis, not a construction cost. In jurisdictions that apply the ICC Building Valuation Data table (including all of Oregon by administrative rule, and many California jurisdictions), the recorded valuation is computed as gross area × a published $/SF rate — it is a formula output, and using it to derive $/SF is circular. Where valuation is applicant-declared, limited public evidence indicates it understates actual construction cost by roughly one-third to one-half. Permit valuation also excludes land and soft costs and is frequently split across shell and tenant-improvement permits. Use it only as a censored lower bound and only after classifying each jurisdiction's valuation method; never as a cost observation."*
