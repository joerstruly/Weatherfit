**Verdict on the structure question:** ~12.5 years × ~150 monthly observations × 4 regions. That is enough to fit a *regional* drift/volatility model and to measure a stable region-vs-national spread. It is **not** enough — and more importantly there is **no data at all** — to fit a *metro*-level model. BLS publishes no metro construction PPI. Also note 236500 is a backward-looking transaction-price index with no forecast component.

# Not found / dead ends (reported prominently)

1. **No free forward construction cost index by US metro exists.** This is the central negative. Mortenson is the only free per-metro index and it is strictly backward-looking quarterly deltas. RSMeans CCI and ENR city indexes are *location factors*, not escalation, and are paid. Nothing free compounds forward at metro level except San Francisco's AICCIE, which covers exactly one city.
2. **BLS publishes no metro or state construction PPI.** 236500 stops at four Census regions; there is no sub-regional cut to fit a metro model against.
3. **BEA publishes no state or metro price deflator for structures** — the NIPA structures deflator is national only.
4. **FTA has no published standard escalation rate** for New Starts/CIG. GAO-23-105479 documents underestimated inflation as a recurring cause of cost growth but prescribes no rate.
5. **No free GSA A/E fee guide with percentages by building type and size.** What exists federally is a 6% statutory ceiling (FAR/VAAR/GSAM) plus worksheet forms 2630/2631.
6. **AIA and ACEC do not publish fee-as-percent-of-construction benchmarks.** AIA publishes the Compensation &amp; Benefits Report (salaries — 13,227 positions in the 2025 edition); ACEC guidance is organized around hourly rate classifications, explicitly *not* phase percentages. Risk #6's gap here is real and confirmed, not a search failure.
7. **No public-agency owner's soft-cost breakdown** (permits, testing, legal, insurance, marketing) as a percentage of hard cost was found. All results were vendor blog content with uncited 20–30% ranges. The closest usable public artifacts are the WA OFM C-100 workbook and the Ohio OFCC fee schedule, which impose a soft-cost *structure* without publishing benchmark percentages.
8. **No Duncan Associates successor.** impactfees.com still hosts the 2019 and 2015 surveys; no organization has picked it up.
9. **No multi-jurisdiction impact fee comparison for the Mountain West** (CO, UT, AZ, ID, NV) after 2019 — only single-jurisdiction studies (Golden Gate FPD 2024, Ada County 2024, Salt Lake City Sept 2025).
10. **Ohio OFCC, Colorado OSA, Wisconsin DFD, Minnesota MMB, Oregon DAS, NY OGS, Hawaii DAGS, Alaska: no published escalation rate found.** Only Maryland, California, Washington, Virginia (mechanism only), Kansas and San Francisco actually publish one.
11. **The Washington CPARB escalation committee was disbanded 2/12/2026** — its Oct 2025 report is a one-off, not a series, and its statutory purpose was small-works-roster thresholds.
12. **Fetch-blocked, unresolved, worth 15 minutes of manual retrieval:** Kansas "Building Cost Projections" page (a live state forward curve, numbers unseen); Massachusetts DCAMM Table I percentages; UFC 3-701-01 Table 4-2 values; the current CWCCIS forward tables on the USACE site; Virginia's CR-1 hard-coded rate; the NAVFAC aeg_fe.pdf fee section; and Construction Analytics' 2026 forecast tables.

# Recommendation — the most defensible free forward escalation curve to a dated midpoint

Build the curve in three explicitly separated layers, and never let the geography layer carry more weight than the evidence supports.

**Layer 1 — method and timing (free, standard, auditable).** Adopt AACE 68R-11 with California DOF BL 26-03's stated convention as the concrete implementation: escalate from the estimate's data date to the **construction midpoint = construction start + (duration in months ÷ 2)**, compounding monthly, and carry escalation as a disclosed line item rather than burying it in the unit rates. BL 26-03 gives you a citable public-agency definition of the midpoint and a monthly compounding rate (0.42%/month), which makes the arithmetic defensible to a reviewer without any proprietary source.

**Layer 2 — the forward national rate, bracketed rather than pointed.** Do not pick one forecaster. Construct the central rate as the median of a small, dated, reproducible panel: the **state capital-budget escalation assumptions** (Maryland DBM's 5.0% CY2026 / 3.5% CY2027-2029; California's ≤5.0%/yr; Washington CPARB's 3.66%–4.9%; San Francisco's 3.5% year-1 then 5.0%), bounded below by the **federal deflator path** (CWCCIS/OMB at ~2.6–2.9%/yr, and the DoD Green Book MILCON deflators) and above by the free private national indexes (Skanska Composite +6% YoY, Cumming ~5%, Gordian/RSMeans ~+2%/quarter). The state assumptions deserve to be the center of gravity for three reasons: they are dated and versioned, they are published by organizations that actually buy construction and are held to their own numbers, and — as Maryland's Spring-to-Fall 2025 revision from 10%/7.5%/5% down to 4.5%/5%/3.5% demonstrates — they are revised on a visible cadence, which lets you audit how the assumption moved and re-run a past estimate against a past vintage. That revision history is itself the honest expression of the ±1.5 pt disagreement flagged in Risk #2: report the escalation line as a range spanning the panel's min and max at your midpoint, not as a single number.

**Layer 3 — geography, as a bounded spread and nothing more.** There is no free forward metro index, so do not manufacture one. Instead apply a **region adjustment measured from BLS PPI 236500** — the trailing 3-to-5-year annualized spread between the relevant Census region series (FRED `PCU236500236500x`, Feb 2014 = 100, monthly, Feb 2014 onward) and the national parent — and, only for metros Mortenson covers, blend in the trailing metro-versus-national spread from the free Mortenson quarterly index. Cap the total geographic adjustment at roughly ±1.0 to ±1.5 percentage points on the annual rate. That cap matters: with only 12 years of four-region monthly history and no metro data at all, the geographic signal is far weaker than the national-rate uncertainty, and an uncapped metro adjustment would let the least-supported layer dominate the most-supported one. Where the project sits in a jurisdiction that publishes its own official rate — San Francisco (AICCIE), Maryland, California, Washington, Kansas — **use that published rate directly in place of Layers 2 and 3**, cite it by document and date, and skip the synthesis entirely. That is the single most defensible position available: a dated, official, geographically specific number that someone else has already had to stand behind.
